## GG3 Cord Mod for Holding Upright and Backwards (HUB System)

*For the latest update, scroll to the end.*

![GRIGRI 3 in the HUB orientation](images/01.jpg)

This post details the HUB method for lead rope soloing (LRS). The acronym means that the GRIGRI is **H**eld **U**pside-down and **B**ackwards relative to its normal orientation on the belay loop when belaying a lead climber. Climbers have done this for years with various methods and modifications. As far as the author knows, the HUB Cord method described here is unique.

The author used four design criteria for attaching the device to a chest harness:

1. The modification must be easy to make.
2. The attachment point on the GRIGRI must be strong and not supported by plastic.
3. The angle at which it hangs from the chest harness must be adjustable.
4. The cord must remain clear of the rope path and GRIGRI handle, where it could interfere with lock-up.

The author uses this arrangement with either a GRIGRI 3 whose cam spring has been deactivated, or a GRIGRI + in top-rope mode. It may be possible to use other GRIGRI models with the spring intact, but the author has not tested this and questions whether they would lock in a high-clip situation. Brent Barghahn has used a GRIGRI 3 in the HUB position without modifying the spring and, as of May 2023, reportedly uses a GRIGRI + in top-rope mode.

> **Important source warning:** Testing by Yann Camus and others indicates that a GRIGRI + in top-rope mode and the HUB position may not always lock when it is loaded slowly in a high-clip situation.

The author considers a springless GRIGRI 3 in the HUB system comparable to a Rock Exotica Soloist or El Mudo, with the additional ability to lower or rappel without adding or exchanging equipment.

## HUB system compared with an inverted, freely hanging GRIGRI

The author considers the inverted, backward-facing GRIGRI and the running cache loop to be two of the best recent innovations for GRIGRI-based LRS. Which arrangement is preferable depends on the climber's priorities and the kind of LRS being done. This post presents the HUB option rather than arguing that either method is universally better.

### HUB system

*Assumes a springless GRIGRI or a GRIGRI + in top-rope mode.*

#### Advantages

- Less possibility of high-clip failure, with quicker and more positive lock-up.
- The GRIGRI is held higher, making it easier to pull slack for clipping, especially for climbers with a short reach.
- The chest harness reduces, but does not eliminate, the tendency to flip backward.
- Less movement of the device during a fall can shorten the fall by as much as one foot. The author considers this important on vertical or lower-angle terrain with objective fall hazards.
- The feed resistance, and therefore backfeed, can be adjusted by changing the GRIGRI angle with the HUB cord.

#### Disadvantages

- It most likely will not arrest a head-first fall unless backed up. The author considers a backup mandatory and says more testing is needed.
- It may feed poorly on traverses and needs more real-world testing.
- It requires a chest or shoulder sling/harness and careful adjustment.
- Pulling the rope directly upward for a high clip immediately locks a springless GRIGRI or GRIGRI + and short-ropes the climber. Instead, pull a loop of slack downward and then bring it up to clip. This becomes natural with practice and resembles pulling slack into a cache loop.

#### Discussion

The author considers the HUB system better suited to vertical or lower-angle routes that do not wander much and may contain objective hazards. It may also suit hangdogging, aid climbing and routes with cruxes close to the ground.

### Inverted GRIGRI hanging backward

#### Advantages

- It may arrest inverted falls.
- Because it hangs freely and can orient itself with the direction of pull, it generally feeds well in different orientations.
- Depending on the rope pairing, the climber may pull rope directly upward to clip.
- The setup is simpler and does not require modification or a chest harness.
- The strong cam spring may produce slightly softer lock-up.

#### Disadvantages

- Lock-up takes longer because the device must rotate 180 degrees and travel from its hanging position to an upright position.
- It is less convenient for short-reach climbers because they must reach farther down for slack.
- Yann Camus and others have demonstrated or experienced failures to lock during high clips or slow weighting without a significant jerk. The strong spring requires a substantial fall or rope impulse to engage.
- Depending on the rope pairing, the device can lock unexpectedly while the climber is clipping.

#### Discussion

The author considers the inverted system better suited to steep or overhanging routes without objective fall hazards, and to climbers concerned about upside-down falls who do not want to add other measures. The setup is generally simpler, but rope compatibility is critical for both feeding and lock-up.

There are nuances to all these claims; the choice is not clear-cut. Test, practice and act safely. As with most soloing devices, device-and-rope compatibility is critical and can greatly affect performance.

## The author's HUB modification

The GRIGRI 3 shown here has had its cam spring deactivated; that modification is covered in the author's [previous post](https://sicgrips.blogspot.com/2022/08/deactivating-spring-in-grigri-3-for.html). Methods used over the years to attach a cord to a reversed, upside-down GRIGRI have ranged from acceptable to awkward to dangerous. The author says this version:

- requires only one drilled hole, which the author claims does not impair the function or integrity of the GRIGRI;
- holds the device at a stable, easily adjustable angle;
- keeps the device attached at one or more points when equipment is exchanged;
- uses the cord connection to help prevent the GRIGRI from cross-loading the carabiner; and
- leaves the GRIGRI functional for normal uses.

### How to make the GG HUB modification (later called the HUBBY system)

HUBBY stands for “Held Upside-Down and Backwards Bridle Yoke.”

1. Drill a small 1/8-inch hole in the cover plate at the location shown. The author claims this does not affect the strength or function of the GRIGRI.
2. Chamfer both sides of the hole so there are no sharp edges to abrade the cord. The author used the tip of a larger drill bit and a small roll of 220-grit emery paper.
3. Pass one end of 2.5 mm accessory cord through the hole. The author later changed to 1.8 mm polyester-covered Dyneema because it is more abrasion resistant and at least as strong; see the GRIGRI + images near the end of the post.
4. Tie an overhand knot in the cord and melt the end into a blob to act as a stopper.
5. The GRIGRI cover plate already fits loosely enough to provide some cord clearance. The author also sanded the plastic spacer to provide more clearance, prevent binding or rubbing on a sharp edge, and let the cover open and close freely.
6. The author originally tied a Poacher's knot—half of a double fisherman's knot—at the carabiner end. This could be loosened to install the carabiner and cinched to center the GRIGRI and limit cross-loading. The author later replaced it with an overhand loop on a bight, as described in an addendum below.
7. Tie an overhand loop on a bight in the middle of the cord as the chest-harness connection. Its position sets the angle at which the GRIGRI hangs and therefore affects rope feed. The appropriate angle depends on the GRIGRI version, rope and harness and must be determined experimentally.

For the author's springless GRIGRI and rope combination, approximately 30–40 degrees from vertical gave the best feeding and lock-up. For a GRIGRI +, the author found 40–50 degrees effective. Other combinations require experimentation.

![HUB orientation and suspension angle](images/03.jpg)

The photographs show the setup paired with a Maxim 9.5 mm Pinnacle rope.

If the GRIGRI feeds too freely, it can cause the live rope to backfeed and pull dangerous slack from the cache loop. Feed resistance can be changed by relocating the knotted suspension loop: moving the device toward vertical increases friction, while moving it toward horizontal decreases friction. The author also refers to [Arctic Bastard's Gromm Hitches](https://youtu.be/otQ2d3PAPBQ) as another backfeed-management method.

## Five ways to manage the cache loop and backup

The source says a cache loop and some form of backup are mandatory for performance and safety. It lists five complete options:

1. Pre-tie cache loops with clove hitches on keyless carabiners clipped to full-strength or backed-up gear loops, as described by [Brent Barghahn](https://www.brentbarghahn.com/climbing-blog/redpoint-rope-soloing-2021). Drop loops as needed.
2. Use a MICRO TRAXION on a carabiner around the waist belt to manage the cache loop, with a separate webbing loop and carabiner near the belay loop. Tie a backup knot between the MICRO TRAXION and GRIGRI when approaching a crux and release it after passing the crux.
3. Use a carabiner around the waist belt with a Klemheist hitch around the rope to manage the cache loop and act as a backup. The author says this requires holding the hitch with the teeth while pulling slack, as with El Mudo.
4. Pre-tie backup slipknots before the cache loop. Pull them free as they approach the MICRO TRAXION. The MICRO TRAXION must be attached to a full-strength or backed-up gear loop.
5. Backpack method: pre-tie backup slipknots at intervals and place them in a backpack. The cache loop feeds from the pack; as each knot appears, grasp it with the mouth and pull it free with the free hand.

## Current thoughts and experiments with a springless GRIGRI in the HUB system

The author continues to experiment with connecting carabiners, cross-loading precautions, and the tension between the chest harness and climbing harness through the GRIGRI and HUB cord. The current preference is to place the weight on the chest or shoulder harness so the device hangs as freely as possible from the HUB cord. A large oval carabiner connects it to the belay loop, allowing some vertical movement and helping preserve the intended angle.

> **Warning:** If the springless GRIGRI moves into a horizontal position, it locks and prevents upward movement.

**Postscript:** When a GRIGRI is used with a cache loop, the author places the loop on the climber's left, beside the moving side plate and rounded lowering lip. A loop on the climber's right can run across the handle and obstruct access during lowering.

An alternative arrangement for either the GRIGRI 3 or GRIGRI + removes the second arm of the cord. It is simpler but holds the device at a slight sideways angle, increasing friction. The hanging angle cannot be adjusted from the chest harness. Feed may still be adequate with smaller ropes, such as diameters below 9.4 mm. The simplified arrangement keeps the HUB cord and chest-harness connection away from the handle.

## Addendum 2: terminology, cord and knot changes

The author renamed the single-leg version the **HUB Tow Cord** and the two-leg version the **HUB Bridle**.

The cord was changed from 2.5 mm accessory cord to 1.8 mm polyester/Dyneema cord, which passes more easily through the gap between the pivoting side plate and device body.

![Updated HUB Bridle construction](images/08.jpg)

The author also changed from a Scaffold knot that cinched onto the carabiner to an overhand loop on a bight, which is easier to install and remove. The loop is sized so the knot clears the GRIGRI body and the tow-loop connection remains more centered.

![Updated overhand loop on a bight](images/09.jpg)

The earlier photographs did not show the chamfer on the inside of the drilled hole. The author uses a drill bit several sizes larger than the hole, under light pressure, to remove the sharp edge and form a shallow bevel that protects the cord.

![Chamfer on the inside of the cord hole](images/10.jpg)

Although some photographs show an aluminum locking carabiner, the author normally uses a rated stainless-steel quick link, an auto-locking steel oval carabiner, or a symmetrical pear-shaped steel carabiner.

## October 2024 update: testing without drilling

![Cord wrapped around the cover-plate pivot, view 1](images/11.jpg)

![Cord wrapped around the cover-plate pivot, view 2](images/12.jpg)

To test the HUB (also called HUR in this update) arrangement with a particular GRIGRI-and-rope combination without drilling a hole, the author suggests starting with approximately three feet of 1.0–1.2 mm Dyneema cord. This is longer than necessary but makes knot tying easier; the final length can be adjusted in the last knot.

1. Wrap the cord around the cover plate's pivot axle and tie a Scaffold knot. Cinch it until the cord seats between the cover plate and body. Depending on side-plate looseness, this may require substantial force.
2. In the middle of the cord, tie a clove hitch around the carabiner used to connect it to the chest harness.
3. In the other end, tie an overhand loop on a bight that slips over the main carabiner.
4. Before dressing and tightening the loop, adjust it to the desired chest-harness suspension length.
5. Trim the excess cord and melt the ends.
6. Adjust the hanging angle by loosening the clove hitch and changing the relative lengths of the two bridle legs.

The author still prefers drilling the cover plate because the cord pulls the plate closed. The GRIGRI has no side-plate lock and can move on the carabiner; in this arrangement, the HUB cord and gravity help press the plate closed.
